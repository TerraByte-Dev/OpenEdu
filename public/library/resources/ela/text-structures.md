# Informational Text Structures

Authors of nonfiction organize ideas in patterns. Recognizing the **structure** — and its **signal words** — improves comprehension.

| Structure | What it does | Signal words |
|---|---|---|
| **Description** | Lists traits, examples, or details about a topic | for example, for instance, such as, characteristics, including |
| **Sequence / Chronological** | Presents events or steps in order | first, next, then, after, finally, before, meanwhile, dates |
| **Compare & Contrast** | Shows how two+ things are alike and different | similarly, likewise, both, in contrast, however, unlike, whereas |
| **Cause & Effect** | Explains why something happens and its results | because, since, therefore, as a result, consequently, leads to, due to |
| **Problem & Solution** | States a problem and proposes answers | problem, issue, solve, solution, propose, in response, answer |

## How to identify the structure
1. Look for **signal words** (above).
2. Ask what the author is *doing*: describing? ordering? comparing? explaining causes? solving?
3. A long text can mix structures — name the **dominant** one for each section.

Matching a graphic organizer to the structure helps: a timeline for sequence, a Venn diagram for compare/contrast, a cause→effect chain, a web for description.
