# Body Systems: Control and Processing

Some systems take in food and clear out waste. Others carry messages, fast through nerves or slower
through the blood. The skin wraps around all of it.

## Digestion

Food travels through one long tube: mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, and
out through the anus. Digestion starts in the mouth, when you chew. Muscles in the walls of the gut
squeeze behind the food and relax in front of it, pushing food along. This is called peristalsis.

The stomach lining makes acid and enzymes that break food down, and the stomach slowly passes the
mixed food on to the small intestine. The small intestine is where most nutrients are taken in: its
walls absorb digested food and water into the blood (digested fats go first into lymph vessels).
The large intestine soaks up water and turns
liquid waste into solid stool.

Three helpers sit outside the tube. The liver makes bile, a juice that helps digest fats, and the
gallbladder stores the bile between meals. The liver is the largest organ inside the body; it helps
digest food, stores energy, and removes poisons. The pancreas makes a digestive juice with enzymes
that break down carbohydrates, fats, and proteins.

- **Mouth** — where digestion starts, when you chew
- **Esophagus** — the part of the tube between the mouth and the stomach
- **Stomach** — the organ whose lining makes acid and enzymes to break food down
- **Small intestine** — where most nutrients are absorbed
- **Large intestine** — soaks up water and turns liquid waste into solid stool
- **Anus** — the opening at the end of the tube, where stool leaves the body
- **Peristalsis** — gut muscles squeezing behind food and relaxing in front of it to push it along
- **Liver** — the largest organ inside the body, which makes bile and removes poisons
- **Gallbladder** — stores bile between meals
- **Bile** — a juice from the liver that helps digest fats
- **Pancreas** — makes a digestive juice with enzymes for carbohydrates, fats and proteins

## Kidneys and urine

The kidneys filter the blood, taking out wastes and extra water to make urine. Healthy kidneys filter
about half a cup of blood every minute. Unfiltered blood flows into each kidney through the renal
artery, and filtered blood leaves through the renal vein. Each kidney has about
a million tiny filters called nephrons. Sources give slightly different counts, so treat that number
as a rough size, not a fact to memorise.

Urine flows from the kidneys down two thin tubes called ureters into the bladder. The bladder stores
urine. Besides removing
waste, the urinary system keeps the body's water level right by changing how much water leaves in
urine.

- **Kidneys** — organs that filter the blood, taking out wastes and extra water
- **Urine** — the liquid of wastes and extra water that the kidneys make
- **Nephrons** — the tiny filters inside each kidney, about a million in each
- **Ureters** — the two thin tubes that carry urine away from the kidneys
- **Bladder** — the organ that stores urine
- **Urinary system** — the system that removes waste and keeps the body's water level right
- **Half a cup** — about how much blood healthy kidneys filter every minute
- **Renal artery** — the blood vessel that brings unfiltered blood into a kidney
- **Renal vein** — the blood vessel that carries filtered blood out of a kidney

## Nervous system

The brain and spinal cord make up the central nervous system; the nerves that reach the rest of the
body make up the peripheral nervous system. Nerve cells, called neurons, do the nervous system's work
by carrying signals called nerve impulses. Sensory neurons carry messages from the senses to the
brain and spinal cord; motor neurons carry orders back out to muscles and glands.

The cerebrum, the largest part of the brain, controls thinking, learning, emotions, speech, and
movements you choose to make. The cerebellum, at the lower back of the brain, coordinates movement
and controls balance and posture. The brain stem joins the brain to the spinal cord and controls breathing and
heart rate.

Many reflexes are handled by the spinal cord without waiting for the brain, so you pull your hand off
something hot before you even feel the pain. The skull protects the brain, and the bones of the spine
(vertebrae) protect the spinal cord.

- **Central nervous system** — the brain and the spinal cord together
- **Peripheral nervous system** — the nerves that reach out to the rest of the body
- **Neurons** — nerve cells, which carry signals called nerve impulses
- **Sensory neurons** — carry messages from the senses to the brain and spinal cord
- **Motor neurons** — carry orders out to muscles and glands
- **Cerebrum** — the largest part of the brain, for thinking, learning, speech and chosen movements
- **Cerebellum** — the part at the lower back of the brain that controls balance and posture
- **Brain stem** — joins the brain to the spinal cord and controls breathing and heart rate
- **Reflex** — a fast, automatic reaction that needs no thinking; many are handled by the spinal cord alone, before you even feel the pain
- **Skull** — the bone that protects the brain
- **Vertebrae** — the bones of the spine, which protect the spinal cord

## Hormones and glands

Hormones are the body's chemical messengers; glands release them into the blood, which carries them
to other organs. Endocrine glands have no tubes (ducts); they send their hormones straight into the
blood. The glands below are some of the main ones, not a full list.

Insulin is a hormone made by the pancreas that helps sugar (glucose) from food get into the body's
cells to be used for energy. The pancreas releases insulin when blood sugar is high and a different
hormone, glucagon, when blood sugar is low. The thyroid is a small butterfly-shaped gland in the
front of the neck; its hormones control how the body uses energy. There is an adrenal gland near the
top of each kidney. It releases adrenaline (epinephrine), especially during stress.

- **Hormones** — the body's chemical messengers, carried in the blood
- **Endocrine glands** — glands with no ducts, which send hormones straight into the blood
- **Insulin** — the hormone that helps sugar from food get into cells for energy
- **Glucagon** — the hormone the pancreas releases when blood sugar is low
- **Glucose** — the sugar from food that insulin helps into the cells
- **Thyroid** — a small butterfly-shaped gland in the front of the neck
- **Adrenal glands** — the glands that sit near the top of each kidney
- **Adrenaline** — a hormone released especially during stress, also called epinephrine

## Skin

The skin is the body's largest organ and forms a barrier against germs and injury. (The liver is the
largest organ inside the body; the skin covers the outside.) The outer skin layer (epidermis) is
tough and protective; the deeper layer (dermis) holds nerve endings, sweat glands, oil glands, and
hair roots.

Sweat glands put sweat onto the skin. As the sweat evaporates (dries), it cools the skin, which helps
keep the body from overheating. What to do when someone does overheat is in the everyday emergencies
unit.

Oil glands in the dermis put an oily substance onto each hair that keeps it from becoming brittle.
Each hair sits in a hair follicle, a tube-shaped sheath that surrounds and feeds the part of the hair
under the skin. A tiny muscle joins each follicle to the skin; when it tightens, the hair stands up
and a goosebump forms.

- **Skin** — the largest organ of the whole body, a barrier against germs and injury
- **Epidermis** — the tough, protective outer layer of skin
- **Dermis** — the deeper skin layer that holds nerve endings, sweat glands, oil glands and hair roots
- **Sweat glands** — glands that put a cooling liquid onto the skin
- **Evaporation** — sweat drying on the skin, which cools it
- **Oil glands** — glands that coat hairs with an oily substance so they do not become brittle
- **Hair follicle** — the tube-shaped sheath that surrounds and feeds the part of a hair under the skin
- **Goosebump** — what forms when a tiny muscle pulls a hair upright
